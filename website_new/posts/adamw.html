Understanding AdamW via Worst-Case Convergence Analysis

Dmitry Kovalev·November 25, 2026
\newcommand{\R}{\mathbb{R}} \newcommand{\E}{\mathbb{E}} \newcommand{\N}{\mathbb{N}} \newcommand{\cD}{\mathcal{D}} \newcommand{\Norm}[1]{\left\lVert #1 \right\rVert} \newcommand{\Abs}[1]{\left\lvert #1 \right\rvert} \newcommand{\norm}[1]{\lVert #1 \rVert} \newcommand{\abs}[1]{\lvert #1 \rvert}

Before the invention of Muon [JJB+24], AdamW [LH19] was arguably the most popular optimizer for large-scale deep learning. Its fast and stable convergence made it a default choice in most practical scenarios. Unfortunately, despite the fact that this algorithm was invented almost a decade ago, its theoretical understanding remains limited. In this post, we will try to find a theoretical justification of AdamW's empirical success by means of theoretical worst-case convergence analysis. More concretely, we will show that AdamW may be the best possible algorithm for a certain class of stochastic convex optimization problems.

Preliminaries

Stochastic Optimization

Given a stochastic objective function \(f_\xi(\cdot)\colon \R^d \to \R\), where \(\xi\) is a random variable sampled from a probability distribution \(\cD\), consider a standard stochastic optimization problem:

\[ \label{eq:main} \min_{x \in \R^d} f(x),\quad\text{where}\quad f(x) = \E_{\xi \sim \cD}[f_\xi(x)]. \]

One of the simplest algorithms for solving such problems is SGD with a constant stepsize \(\eta > 0\), which iterates

\[ x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta \cdot \nabla f_{\xi_k}(x_k), \]

where \(\xi_k \sim \cD\) is sampled independently at each iteration. Under mild regularity assumptions, the stochastic gradient is unbiased, i.e.,

\[ \E [\nabla f_{\xi_k}(x_k) \mid x_k] = \nabla f(x_k), \]

so that SGD recovers the full-batch GD update in expectation.

AdamW Recap

AdamW is an extension of SGD, its pseudocode is given below. Here, \(\odot\) and \(\oslash\) denote the elementwise multiplication and division, respectively.

Algorithm 1: AdamW

  • input: \(x_1 \in \R^d\), \((m_0,v_0) = 0 \in \R\times\R^d\)
  • for \(k = 1,2, \dots, K-1\)
    • obtain a stochastic gradient:
      \(g_k = \nabla f_{\xi_k}(x_k)\), where \(\xi_k \sim \cD\) is a fresh sample
    • compute the 1st moment:
      \(m_k = \beta_1 m_{k-1} + (1-\beta_1) g_k\)
    • compute the 2nd moment:
      \(v_k = \beta_2 v_{k-1} + (1-\beta_2) (g_k \odot g_k)\)
    • apply bias correction:
      \(\hat{m}_k = m_k/(1-\beta_1^k)\), \(\hat{v}_k = v_k/(1-\beta_2^k)\)
    • perform an update with elementwise preconditioning and decoupled weight decay:
      \(x_{k+1} = (1-\eta\lambda)x_k - \eta \cdot \hat{m}_k\oslash(\sqrt{\hat v_k} + \epsilon)\)
  • output: \(x_K\)

AdamW can be seen as a combination of the following algorithmic components:

  • the 1st and 2nd gradient moments,
  • bias correction,
  • elementwise preconditioning,
  • decoupled weight decay.

The first three were already present in the original Adam optimizer [KB15]; the last one, decoupled weight decay, is the letter "W" that was added to Adam in the original AdamW paper [LH19]. In this blog post we deliberately leave out of the consideration the bias correction and elementwise preconditioning; below, we discuss the reasons.

Bias correction ensures that the corrected 1st moment estimate \(\hat{m}_k\) is a convex combination of the past gradients \(g_1,\dots,g_k\). Its original justification is based on the following observation: if the distribution of the gradients is stationary, implying \(\E[g_j] = \mu\) for all \(j \in \N\), then we also have \(\E[\hat{m}_k] = \mu\); similar intuition applies to the corrected second moment \(\hat{v}_k\). It can also be seen as using a time-varying momentum parameter or a stepsize schedule. Despite these interpretaions, the optimization benefits of the bias correction are not guaranteed. In particular, recent empirical studies [LO25] suggest that the bias correction can be removed, especially when \(\beta_1\approx\beta_2\) or certain stepsize schedules are used. Therefore, we set aside this mechanism, although we acknowledge that it deserves further theoretical investigation.

Elementwise preconditioning in AdamW corresponds to the elementwise division of the update by the square root of the corrected second moment. Unlike bias correction, its role is relatively well understood: it is effective when different coordinates affect the objective function at very different scales. Intuitively, more sensitive coordinates tend to have larger gradient components; elementwise normalization by the square root of the 2nd gradient moment helps to balance the update magnitudes across coordinates. The benefits of such preconditioning have also received a formal theoretical justification via convergence bounds for AdaGrad [DHS11; see also Kov26]. Therefore, in this post, we set aside the elementwise preconditioning and focus on a scalar-stepsize variant of AdamW. Importantly, the preconditioning analysis of [DHS11 Kov26] can be readily combined with the scalar-stepsize analysis developed in this post.
